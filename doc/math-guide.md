# The mathematics of the hyperbolic tiling viewer

This guide develops, from linear algebra up, all the mathematics the viewer
uses: the hyperboloid model of the hyperbolic plane, its isometries, the
Poincaré disk, hyperbolic trigonometry, triangle reflection groups and the
folding algorithm, holonomy, and the finite quotients used for tile colors.
It assumes comfort with groups, presentations, group actions, Coxeter
groups at the level of "a group generated by involutions with relations
$(s_is_j)^{m_{ij}}=1$", and basic finite fields. It does not assume any
hyperbolic geometry.

Sections 1–9 develop the hyperbolic case. Section 10 extends the model, the
picture and the colors to the Euclidean and spherical tilings, which the code
handles in the same formulas, parametrized by the curvature $\kappa$.
Section 11 adds infinite $p$ and $q$, and section 12 the hyperideal labels
beyond them. Section 13 adds two more conformal pictures, the half-plane and
the band, and section 14 a view of the surface itself in 3D.

Each section ends with the places in the code where the material is used,
and with a few exercises. Formulas that appear in the code are marked
**(code)**.

Notation used throughout:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\langle a,b\rangle$ | Lorentz form $-a_0b_0+a_1b_1+a_2b_2$ |
| $J$ | $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1)$, so $\langle a,b\rangle=a^{\mathsf T}Jb$ |
| $\mathbb H^2$ | the hyperboloid $\{x:\langle x,x\rangle=-1,\ x_0>0\}$ |
| $O$ | the origin $(1,0,0)\in\mathbb H^2$ |
| $\{p,q\}$ | regular tiling by $p$-gons, $q$ meeting at each vertex |
| $\Delta$ | the triangle group $\Delta(p,q,2)$ generated by reflections $a,b,c$ |
| $D$ | the fundamental triangle $OVE$ |
| $\kappa$, $K$ | curvature $-1,0,1$, and $\operatorname{diag}(\kappa,1,1)$ (§10) |

## 1. Minkowski space and the hyperboloid

### 1.1 The Lorentz form

Let $\mathbb R^{2,1}$ be $\mathbb R^3$ with the symmetric bilinear form
$$\langle a,b\rangle=-a_0b_0+a_1b_1+a_2b_2 .$$
It is non-degenerate of signature $(2,1)$: two plus signs and one minus.
Vectors split into three kinds:

* **timelike**, $\langle v,v\rangle<0$;
* **lightlike** (or null), $\langle v,v\rangle=0$, $v\neq0$, forming the
  light cone $x_0^2=x_1^2+x_2^2$;
* **spacelike**, $\langle v,v\rangle>0$.

The set $\langle x,x\rangle=-1$ is a two-sheeted hyperboloid
$x_0^2=1+x_1^2+x_2^2$. The upper sheet, $x_0>0$, is our model of the
hyperbolic plane:
$$\mathbb H^2=\{x\in\mathbb R^{2,1}:\langle x,x\rangle=-1,\ x_0>0\}.$$

### 1.2 Why this is a Riemannian surface

Differentiating $\langle x,x\rangle=-1$ shows that the tangent plane at
$x\in\mathbb H^2$ is $T_x=x^\perp=\{v:\langle x,v\rangle=0\}$, the
Lorentz-orthogonal complement. The key linear-algebra fact:

> **Lemma.** If $x$ is timelike then $x^\perp$ is spacelike, i.e. the form is
> positive definite on $x^\perp$.

*Proof.* The form has exactly one negative direction (Sylvester's law of
inertia). If $x^\perp$ contained a vector $v$ with $\langle v,v\rangle\le0$,
then $\operatorname{span}(x,v)$ would be a 2-dimensional subspace on which the
form is negative semidefinite, which contradicts signature $(2,1)$. $\square$

So restricting $\langle\,,\rangle$ to tangent planes gives a Riemannian metric
on $\mathbb H^2$. That metric is the hyperbolic metric, and it has constant
curvature $-1$. You can take that as a definition. It is also what the
trigonometry in §4 encodes.

### 1.3 Distance

> **Reverse Cauchy–Schwarz.** For timelike $x,y$ in the same sheet,
> $\langle x,y\rangle\le-\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle\langle y,y\rangle}$, with
> equality iff $x,y$ are proportional.

For points of $\mathbb H^2$ this says $-\langle x,y\rangle\ge1$, and the
hyperbolic distance is
$$\cosh d(x,y)=-\langle x,y\rangle. \qquad\textbf{(code)}$$
The curve $\gamma(t)=\cosh t\,x+\sinh t\,u$, for $x\in\mathbb H^2$ and $u$ a
unit tangent vector at $x$, lies on $\mathbb H^2$, has unit speed, and
satisfies $-\langle x,\gamma(t)\rangle=\cosh t$. It is the geodesic through $x$
in direction $u$. It is also the intersection of $\mathbb H^2$ with the plane
$\operatorname{span}(x,u)$.

### 1.4 Lines and their normals

> **Geodesics are exactly the intersections of $\mathbb H^2$ with planes
> through the origin that meet it.** Such a plane is $n^\perp$ for a
> spacelike $n$, which we normalise to $\langle n,n\rangle=1$.

So a **line** is determined by a unit spacelike vector $n$ up to sign. The
sign picks a side: the two half-planes are $\langle x,n\rangle>0$ and
$\langle x,n\rangle<0$. In the code, every mirror is stored as the unit normal
pointing *into* the fundamental triangle.

The most useful single identity in the whole program:

> **Signed distance to a line.** For $x\in\mathbb H^2$ and unit spacelike $n$,
> $$\langle x,n\rangle=\sinh(\text{signed distance from }x\text{ to the line }n^\perp).\qquad\textbf{(code)}$$

*Proof sketch.* Let $y$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $x$ to the
line. The geodesic from $y$ to $x$ has initial direction $\pm n$, because $n$
is orthogonal to the line and tangent at $y$ (it lies in $y^\perp$). So
$x=\cosh d\,y\pm\sinh d\,n$. Take $\langle\cdot,n\rangle$ and use
$\langle y,n\rangle=0$, $\langle n,n\rangle=1$. $\square$

The shader uses this twice. The fold tests the sign of $\langle t,n\rangle$,
and edge lines are drawn where $\operatorname{asinh}|\langle t,n_c\rangle|$ is
small.

**Example (code).** The line at distance $r$ from $O$, perpendicular to the
Euclidean unit direction $u\in\mathbb R^2$, has unit normal
$$n=\pm(\sinh r,\ \cosh r\,u).$$
Check: $\langle n,n\rangle=-\sinh^2r+\cosh^2r=1$, and
$\langle O,n\rangle=\mp\sinh r$. The third mirror $n_c$ in the code is exactly
this, with $r$ the inradius of the tile and $u$ the direction of the edge
midpoint $E$. See §4.3.

### 1.5 Angles between lines

Two distinct lines $n_1^\perp,n_2^\perp$ intersect in $\mathbb H^2$ iff
$|\langle n_1,n_2\rangle|<1$. In that case
$$\langle n_1,n_2\rangle=-\cos\theta,$$
where $\theta$ is the angle *of the region* $\{\langle x,n_1\rangle\ge0,\ \langle x,n_2\rangle\ge0\}$
at the intersection point. This is the reason for the sign convention
"inward normals have $\langle n_i,n_j\rangle=-\cos(\pi/m_{ij})$". At the
intersection point $y$, both normals lie in the tangent plane $y^\perp$, where
the form is Euclidean, so this is the ordinary statement that inward normals
of a wedge of angle $\theta$ make an angle $\pi-\theta$.

If $|\langle n_1,n_2\rangle|=1$ the lines are *asymptotically parallel*: they
meet at a point of the light cone, an "ideal point at infinity". If
$|\langle n_1,n_2\rangle|>1$ they are *ultraparallel*, with a unique common
perpendicular at distance $\operatorname{arccosh}|\langle n_1,n_2\rangle|$.

**Duality.** Points of $\mathbb H^2$ are unit timelike vectors and lines are
unit spacelike vectors. The same Gram-matrix language describes both:
point–point $\leftrightarrow$ $\cosh$ of distance, point–line
$\leftrightarrow$ $\sinh$ of distance, line–line $\leftrightarrow$ $\cos$ of
angle, or $\cosh$ of distance if ultraparallel.

**In the code:** `Geometry::form`, which for $\kappa=-1$ is this form
applied to covectors (§10.1); the edge and vertex distances in `fs`.

**Exercises.**
1. Prove reverse Cauchy–Schwarz. Hint: write $y=\alpha x+w$ with
   $w\in x^\perp$ and use the Lemma.
2. Show that the curve $\gamma(t)$ above has unit speed and that
   $d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=|s-t|$.
3. Show that two lines $n_1^\perp,n_2^\perp$ meet in $\mathbb H^2$ iff the
   plane $\operatorname{span}(n_1,n_2)$ has signature $(2,0)$, i.e. its Gram
   determinant $1-\langle n_1,n_2\rangle^2$ is positive.

## 2. Isometries

### 2.1 The Lorentz group

A linear map $M$ preserving $\langle\,,\rangle$ satisfies $M^{\mathsf T}JM=J$.
These maps form $O(2,1)$. Those that also preserve the upper sheet form
$O^+(2,1)$, and $O^+(2,1)$ is exactly the isometry group of $\mathbb H^2$. In
the sense of having all the isometries; we will not need the proof that there
are no non-linear ones. The orientation-preserving subgroup is
$SO^+(2,1)\cong PSL(2,\mathbb R)$.

Inverting is cheap, because $M^{\mathsf T}JM=J$ gives
$$M^{-1}=JM^{\mathsf T}J.$$
Unlike a general inverse, this is exact and numerically harmless. The code
now uses a general inverse, because the formula has no Euclidean analogue
(§10.3); `settle` keeps $M$ well-conditioned, so nothing is lost.

### 2.2 Reflections

For unit spacelike $n$, the reflection in the line $n^\perp$ is
$$\sigma_n(x)=x-2\langle x,n\rangle n,\qquad \sigma_n=I-2\,n\,(Jn)^{\mathsf T}.\qquad\textbf{(code: `Geometry::reflect`, with covector $Jn$)}$$
It fixes $n^\perp$ pointwise, sends $n\mapsto-n$, is an involution and
preserves the form. It also maps the upper sheet to itself: it fixes points of
the line, and the sheets are connected components.

**Cartan–Dieudonné** (in dimension 3): every element of $O(2,1)$ is a product
of at most 3 reflections. Every isometry of $\mathbb H^2$ is therefore a
product of at most three reflections in lines.

### 2.3 Rotations and translations from two reflections

Let $\ell_1,\ell_2$ be lines.

* If they meet at $y$ at angle $\theta$, then $\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}$ is a
  **rotation about $y$ by $2\theta$**, exactly as in the Euclidean plane,
  because at $y$ the tangent plane is Euclidean.
* If they are ultraparallel at distance $\delta$, then $\sigma_2\sigma_1$ is a
  **translation by $2\delta$ along their common perpendicular**. It fixes no
  point of $\mathbb H^2$, preserves the common perpendicular, and shifts it.
* If they are asymptotically parallel, the product is **parabolic**: it fixes
  one ideal point and no point of $\mathbb H^2$. The Euclidean plane has no
  analogue.

The rotation about $O$ by $\alpha$ is
$$R(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\0&\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}.\qquad\textbf{(code: `geom::rotate`)}$$

**The translation used for panning.** We want the
translation carrying $p$ to $q$ along the geodesic $pq$, with no rotational
part. Write it as two reflections in lines perpendicular to $pq$: first the
line through $p$, then the perpendicular bisector of $pq$. Their distance is
$d/2$, so the product translates by $d$ along $pq$. The first reflection fixes
$p$, and the second swaps $p$ and $q$, so $p\mapsto q$.

Normals: let $c=\cosh d=-\langle p,q\rangle$.

* The unit tangent at $p$ pointing to $q$ is $n_1=(q-cp)/\sqrt{c^2-1}$. It is
  orthogonal to $p$ because $\langle q-cp,p\rangle=-c+c=0$, and its norm is
  $\langle q,q\rangle-2c\langle p,q\rangle+c^2\langle p,p\rangle=-1+2c^2-c^2=c^2-1$.
  As a *normal*, it defines the line through $p$ perpendicular to $pq$.
* The bisector's normal is $n_2=(q-p)/\sqrt{2(c-1)}$, since
  $\langle q-p,q-p\rangle=-2+2c$. Its line is $\{x:\langle x,p\rangle=\langle x,q\rangle\}$,
  the points equidistant from $p$ and $q$.

So $T_{p\to q}=\sigma_{n_2}\sigma_{n_1}$.

A translation along a geodesic is a *pure* translation, so
$T_{q\to p}T_{p\to q}=I$. That is false for, say, $T_{p\to q}$ composed with a
rotation about $q$ (unit test: `translate_carries_p_to_q`). The code
(`Geometry::translate`) builds the same matrix another way, which also works
for the other curvatures (§10.3).

### 2.4 Classification by trace

For $M\in SO^+(2,1)$, $\operatorname{tr}M=1+2\cos\alpha$ for a rotation by
$\alpha$, $\operatorname{tr}M=3$ for parabolics, and
$\operatorname{tr}M=1+2\cosh\delta$ for a translation by $\delta$. Every
non-identity element is exactly one of elliptic, parabolic or hyperbolic. This
is the 3×3 version of the familiar $|\operatorname{tr}|<2$, $=2$, $>2$ for
$PSL(2,\mathbb R)$.

### 2.5 Keeping a Lorentz matrix Lorentz

A matrix $M$ is in $O(2,1)$ iff its columns $c_0,c_1,c_2$ satisfy
$\langle c_i,c_j\rangle=J_{ij}$, i.e. they form a *Lorentz-orthonormal* basis.
After many floating-point products this drifts. **Lorentzian Gram–Schmidt**
repairs it. (The code now uses a factorization that works for every
curvature, §10.3.)
$$c_0\leftarrow \frac{c_0}{\sqrt{-\langle c_0,c_0\rangle}},\quad
c_1\leftarrow c_1+\langle c_1,c_0\rangle c_0,\ \ c_1\leftarrow\frac{c_1}{\sqrt{\langle c_1,c_1\rangle}},\quad
c_2\leftarrow c_2+\langle c_2,c_0\rangle c_0-\langle c_2,c_1\rangle c_1,\ \ c_2\leftarrow\frac{c_2}{\sqrt{\langle c_2,c_2\rangle}}.$$
The plus sign on the $c_0$ projection is because $\langle c_0,c_0\rangle=-1$.
The projection of $v$ onto $c_0$ is
$\frac{\langle v,c_0\rangle}{\langle c_0,c_0\rangle}c_0=-\langle v,c_0\rangle c_0$.

**Exercises.**
1. Show $M^{\mathsf T}JM=J$ implies $MJM^{\mathsf T}=J$, so the rows are also
   Lorentz-orthonormal.
2. Show that $\sigma_{n_2}\sigma_{n_1}$ for $\langle n_1,n_2\rangle=-\cos\theta$
   has trace $1+2\cos 2\theta$.
3. Compute $T_{O\to x}$ for $x=(\cosh d,\sinh d,0)$ and check it is the
   "boost" $\begin{pmatrix}\cosh d&\sinh d&0\\\sinh d&\cosh d&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$.
4. Show that boosts are symmetric matrices. Hence a bug that transposes the
   matrix sent to the GPU would be invisible while you only pan, and would
   only show up as reversed rotation.

## 3. The Poincaré disk

### 3.1 Stereographic projection from $(-1,0,0)$

Project $\mathbb H^2$ to the disk $x_0=0$, $u^2+v^2<1$, from the point
$S=(-1,0,0)$, the bottom of the lower sheet. The line from $S$ through
$x\in\mathbb H^2$ meets $x_0=0$ at
$$(u,v)=\frac{(x_1,x_2)}{1+x_0},\qquad\textbf{(code: `point\_to\_disk`)}$$
with inverse
$$x=\frac{(1+\rho^2,\ 2u,\ 2v)}{1-\rho^2},\quad\rho^2=u^2+v^2.\qquad\textbf{(code: `disk\_to\_point`, shader)}$$
The origin $O$ maps to the disk centre. As $x$ goes to infinity on the
hyperboloid, $\rho\to1$.

### 3.2 The metric and conformality

Pulling back the metric gives
$$ds=\frac{2\,|d\mathbf u|}{1-\rho^2}.$$
It is a scalar multiple of the Euclidean metric at each point, so the model is
**conformal**: Euclidean angles in the disk are true hyperbolic angles. This
is why the tiling "looks right" locally: polygon corners really
do appear with angle $2\pi/q$.

The shader uses the conformal factor to convert one pixel into hyperbolic
length:
$$\text{px}_{\mathbb H}=\frac{2}{R_{\text{disk}}\,(1-\rho^2)},\qquad\textbf{(code)}$$
and draws lines of a fixed *pixel* width by thresholding hyperbolic distance
at a multiple of this. Near the rim one pixel is a huge hyperbolic distance,
so tiles become sub-pixel.

### 3.3 Geodesics in the disk

A line $n^\perp$ pulls back to a circle arc orthogonal to the unit circle, or a
diameter if $n_0=0$. One way to see it: substitute the inverse map into
$\langle x,n\rangle=0$ to get
$-n_0(1+\rho^2)+2n_1u+2n_2v=0$, which is a circle, and check orthogonality to
$\rho=1$. This was the visual test for milestone 2.

Isometries become Möbius transformations of the disk. We never need that
form, because all computation happens on the hyperboloid, where isometries are
linear. The disk is only the final projection to pixels.

### 3.4 The Klein model, and why not

Projecting from the origin instead, $(u,v)=(x_1,x_2)/x_0$, gives the Klein
disk. Lines become straight chords, which is attractive, but the model is not
conformal and angles are distorted. Poincaré is the default; the panel can
switch to Klein, or anything between Klein and the orthographic projection
(§10.7).

### 3.5 Growth, and what it does to floating point

A hyperbolic circle of radius $r$ has circumference $2\pi\sinh r$ and area
$2\pi(\cosh r-1)$. Both grow like $e^r$. Three consequences for the
implementation:

* At disk radius $\rho$, the hyperbolic distance from the centre is
  $r=2\operatorname{artanh}\rho$. At $\rho^2=0.999$, $r\approx7.6$. There are
  exponentially many tiles within that radius, which is why the rim looks
  like dust.
* Hyperboloid coordinates there are about $2/(1-\rho^2)=2000$. A float32 has
  24 bits of mantissa, so relative error $\sim6\cdot10^{-8}$ becomes an
  absolute error $\sim10^{-4}$ in coordinates where the quantities we threshold
  are $\sinh$ of distances $\ll1$. That is the precise sense in which "f32
  gives out at the rim", and why the shader fades it.
* Entries of the view matrix $M$ grow like $e^{d}$ with the distance $d$ the
  view has been panned. Without §7's rebasing, $M$ would lose precision and
  eventually overflow. The unit test `settle_preserves_picture_and_bounds_m`
  shows the uncorrected matrix exceeding 20 after only 50 small steps.

**Exercises.**
1. Derive $ds=2|d\mathbf u|/(1-\rho^2)$ from the inverse map.
2. Show that the disk image of $n^\perp$ is orthogonal to the unit circle.
3. Show $d(O,x)=2\operatorname{artanh}\rho$ where $\rho$ is the disk radius of $x$.

## 4. Hyperbolic trigonometry and the fundamental triangle

### 4.1 Right triangles

For a geodesic triangle with a right angle at $C$, legs $a,b$ opposite angles
$A,B$, and hypotenuse $c$:
$$\cosh c=\cosh a\cosh b,\qquad \cosh c=\cot A\cot B,\qquad \cos A=\cosh a\,\sin B,$$
$$\sinh a=\sinh c\,\sin A,\qquad \tanh b=\tanh c\,\cos A,\qquad \tanh a=\sinh b\,\tan A.$$
The first is the hyperbolic Pythagoras theorem. For small triangles all of
these reduce to their Euclidean versions; $\cosh c=\cot A\cot B$ degenerates
to $A+B=\pi/2$.

All of them follow from the Gram matrix of the three side normals. A neat
uniform derivation is in Ratcliffe, *Foundations of Hyperbolic Manifolds*,
chapter 3. They are worth deriving at least once using §1's dictionary: the Gram
matrix of the three inward normals, with entries $1$ on the diagonal and
$-\cos(\text{angle})$ off it, determines the triangle up to isometry. Its
inverse gives the vertices.

### 4.2 Angle defect and area

> **Gauss–Bonnet for geodesic triangles** (curvature $-1$):
> $$\operatorname{Area}=\pi-(A+B+C).$$

So hyperbolic triangles have angle sum $<\pi$, and any triple of angles with
sum $<\pi$ is realized by a unique triangle up to isometry. Polygons:
$\operatorname{Area}=(n-2)\pi-\sum\text{angles}$.

### 4.3 The triangle $OVE$ of the $\{p,q\}$ tiling

Subdivide a regular $p$-gon tile barycentrically into $2p$ triangles. Each one
has vertices

* $O$, the tile centre, with angle $\pi/p$;
* $V$, a polygon vertex, with angle $\pi/q$, since $q$ tiles meet there and
  each contributes two triangles;
* $E$, an edge midpoint, with angle $\pi/2$.

Such a triangle exists iff $\frac\pi p+\frac\pi q+\frac\pi2<\pi$, i.e.
$\frac1p+\frac1q<\frac12$, i.e. $(p-2)(q-2)>4$. Equality is the Euclidean
case: $\{4,4\},\{3,6\},\{6,3\}$. "Greater than $\pi$" is the spherical case:
the Platonic solids. **(code: `Tiling::new`)**

Applying 4.1 with $C=E$, $A=O$, $B=V$:

* hypotenuse $|OV|=R$, the circumradius: $\cosh R=\cot\frac\pi p\cot\frac\pi q$
  **(code)**, so $V=(\cosh R,\sinh R,0)$ when $V$ is placed on the $x_1$ axis;
* leg $|VE|=s$, half the edge: $\cos\frac\pi p=\cosh s\sin\frac\pi q$, so
  $\cosh s=\cos\frac\pi p/\sin\frac\pi q$ **(code: `half_edge`)**;
* leg $|OE|=r$, the inradius: $\cosh r=\cos\frac\pi q/\sin\frac\pi p$.

The three inward normals of $D=OVE$ are:

* $n_a=(0,0,1)$: the line $OV$, the $x_1$ axis; inside is $x_2>0$;
* $n_b=(0,\sin\frac\pi p,-\cos\frac\pi p)$: the line $OE$ through $O$ at angle
  $\pi/p$;
* $n_c=-(\sinh r,\ \cosh r\,\cos\tfrac\pi p,\ \cosh r\,\sin\tfrac\pi p)$: the
  line $VE$, perpendicular to $OE$ at distance $r$ (§1.4's example).

Using $\cosh r=\cos\frac\pi q/\sin\frac\pi p$ this is exactly the handoff's
$$n_c=\Bigl(-\sqrt{\tfrac{\cos^2(\pi/q)}{\sin^2(\pi/p)}-1},\ -\cos\tfrac\pi q\cot\tfrac\pi p,\ -\cos\tfrac\pi q\Bigr).$$
Their Gram matrix is
$$B=\bigl(\langle n_i,n_j\rangle\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\cos\frac\pi p&-\cos\frac\pi q\\-\cos\frac\pi p&1&0\\-\cos\frac\pi q&0&1\end{pmatrix},$$
as required by §1.5 for angles $\pi/p$ at $O$ (between $a$ and $b$), $\pi/q$
at $V$ (between $a$ and $c$) and $\pi/2$ at $E$ (between $b$ and $c$). **(code
test: `gram_conditions`)**

$\det B=1-\cos^2\frac\pi p-\cos^2\frac\pi q$. This is negative exactly in the
hyperbolic case, zero in the Euclidean case and positive in the spherical
case: $B$ has signature $(2,1)$, $(2,0)$ or $(3,0)$. The Gram matrix knows
which geometry it lives in.

Areas: $\operatorname{Area}(D)=\frac\pi2-\frac\pi p-\frac\pi q$, and a tile has
area $2p$ times that, $(p-2)\pi-\frac{2p\pi}q$.

**Exercises.**
1. Verify $\langle n_c,V\rangle=0$ and $\langle n_a,V\rangle=0$ directly.
2. Compute the vertex $E$ as the unit timelike vector orthogonal to $n_b,n_c$
   (it is $\propto J(n_b\times n_c)$, which is how the sweep test finds it), and
   check $\cosh|OE|=\cos\frac\pi q/\sin\frac\pi p$.
3. Show that among all hyperbolic $\{p,q\}$ the triangle $D$ is smallest for
   $\{7,3\}$ and $\{3,7\}$ (the $(2,3,7)$ triangle, area $\pi/42$). Relate this
   to Hurwitz's bound $84(g-1)$ on the order of the automorphism group of a
   closed Riemann surface of genus $g$.

## 5. The triangle reflection group

### 5.1 Poincaré's theorem and the presentation

Let $a,b,c$ be the reflections in the sides $OV$, $OE$, $VE$ of $D$.

> **Poincaré's polygon theorem** (special case). If a compact geodesic
> polygon has all its angles of the form $\pi/m$, the reflections in its sides
> generate a discrete group $\Gamma$, the images of the polygon tile
> $\mathbb H^2$ (images of the interior are disjoint), and $\Gamma$ has the
> presentation "the generators are involutions, and for each vertex of angle
> $\pi/m$ between sides $s,t$, $(st)^m=1$".

For our triangle:
$$\Delta=\Delta(p,q,2)=\langle a,b,c\mid a^2,b^2,c^2,(ab)^p,(ac)^q,(bc)^2\rangle,$$
a Coxeter group with Coxeter diagram $\;\overset{}{b}\overset{p}{\text{—}}a\overset{q}{\text{—}}c\;$,
$b$ and $c$ commuting. The rotation subgroup $\Delta^+$, of index 2, is the
von Dyck group $\langle x,y\mid x^p=y^q=(xy)^2=1\rangle$, with $x=ab$, $y=ac$.
$\Delta$ is also the full symmetry group of the $\{p,q\}$ tiling.

### 5.2 Cells as cosets

Since $\Delta$ acts simply transitively on the triangles (chambers), triangles
$\leftrightarrow$ elements $g$ via $g\mapsto gD$. The other cells are cosets of
the vertex stabilizers of $D$:

| cell of the tiling | vertex of $D$ | stabilizer | cells $\leftrightarrow$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| tile (face) | $O$ | $\langle a,b\rangle\cong D_p$ (dihedral, order $2p$) | $\Delta/\langle a,b\rangle$ |
| vertex | $V$ | $\langle a,c\rangle\cong D_q$ (order $2q$) | $\Delta/\langle a,c\rangle$ |
| edge | $E$ | $\langle b,c\rangle\cong(\mathbb Z/2)^2$ | $\Delta/\langle b,c\rangle$ |

Incidence is "the cosets intersect". This is the Coxeter-complex, or
coset-geometry, view of the tiling, which you may know from buildings or
abstract polytopes.

### 5.3 Colorings are equivariant maps

A coloring of tiles that is *defined from the group* is a
$\Delta$-equivariant map $\Delta/\langle a,b\rangle\to X$ to some $\Delta$-set
$X$. Equivalently it is a point $x_0\in X$ fixed by $\langle a,b\rangle$; the
tile $g\langle a,b\rangle$ gets color $g\cdot x_0$. Given a homomorphism
$\varphi:\Delta\to H$ and an action of $H$ on $X$, this needs
$\varphi(a)x_0=\varphi(b)x_0=x_0$, and nothing else. In particular **no
canonical form for $g$ is needed**: any word for $g$ gives the same
$\varphi(g)$. §8 builds such a $\varphi$ to a finite group for every $\{p,q\}$.

The simplest example is the sign character $\varepsilon:\Delta\to\{\pm1\}$,
$a,b,c\mapsto-1$. It is well defined because every relator has even length,
and it gives the two-tone checkerboard of *triangles* (parity in the shader).
It is not a tile coloring, since $a$ and $b$ do not fix a point of
$\{\pm1\}$. That is the problem milestone 6 set out to solve.

### 5.4 Coxeter's geometric representation

For a Coxeter system with matrix $(m_{ij})$, let $V=\mathbb R^3$ with basis
$e_a,e_b,e_c$ and bilinear form $B(e_i,e_j)=-\cos(\pi/m_{ij})$, and define
$$\sigma_i(v)=v-2B(e_i,v)\,e_i.$$
Then $s_i\mapsto\sigma_i$ is a faithful representation $\Delta\to O(V,B)$
(Tits). In our case $B$ is the matrix of §4.3, of signature $(2,1)$. The map
$e_i\mapsto n_i$ is an isometry $(V,B)\to\mathbb R^{2,1}$, which carries
$\sigma_i$ to our reflection in $n_i$. **The geometric representation is our
Lorentz representation, written in the basis of mirror normals.** §8 uses this
basis because its matrix entries are algebraic integers:
$$2B=\begin{pmatrix}2&-\lambda_p&-\lambda_q\\-\lambda_p&2&0\\-\lambda_q&0&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda_m=2\cos\tfrac\pi m.$$

**Exercises.**
1. Compute the abelianization of $\Delta(p,q,2)$. Deduce that the tiles of

   to $\mathbb Z/2$ killing $a,b$ iff $q$ is even. Check this against
   pictures of $\{4,5\}$ and $\{5,4\}$.
2. Show that the stabilizer of the edge midpoint $E$ is $\langle b,c\rangle$
   and that it is a Klein four-group, not cyclic.
3. Identify the vertex links: the $2q$ triangles around $V$ are
   $\langle a,c\rangle D$. Show this is consistent with the angle $\pi/q$ at $V$.

## 6. The fold: reduction to the fundamental triangle

### 6.1 The algorithm

Given $x\in\mathbb H^2$, repeat: if $\langle x,n_k\rangle<0$ for some mirror
$k$, replace $x$ by $\sigma_k x$. Stop when $x\in D$. **(code: `Tiling::fold`,
shader `fs`)** The output is the folded point $t\in D$ and the word
$k_1k_2\cdots k_\ell$ of reflections applied, so that
$$t=s_{k_\ell}\cdots s_{k_1}x,\qquad x\in gD\ \text{with}\ g=s_{k_1}s_{k_2}\cdots s_{k_\ell}.$$

### 6.2 Why it terminates, and what it computes

The images of the three mirrors under $\Delta$ form a locally finite family of
lines, the **walls**. For a chamber $gD$, let $\ell(g)$ be the number of walls
separating $gD$ from $D$. It is a standard Coxeter-group fact that this equals
the **word length** of $g$ in the generators $a,b,c$.

> **Step lemma.** If $x\in gD$ and $x$ is on the wrong side of wall
> $n_k^\perp$ of $D$, then $s_kx\in s_kgD$ and $\ell(s_kg)=\ell(g)-1$.

*Proof.* Write $W(g)$ for the set of walls separating $D$ from $gD$, and
$H_k=n_k^\perp$. By hypothesis $H_k\in W(g)$. The reflection $s_k$ maps
$W(g)$ bijectively onto the set of walls separating $s_kD$ from $s_kgD$. For a
wall $H\ne H_k$, the chambers $D$ and $s_kD$ lie on the same side of $H$,
since they are adjacent across $H_k$ only. So $H$ separates $s_kD$ from
$s_kgD$ iff it separates $D$ from $s_kgD$. The wall $H_k$ separates $s_kD$ from
$s_kgD$, but $s_kgD$ lies on $D$'s side of $H_k$. Hence
$W(s_kg)=s_kW(g)\setminus\{H_k\}$ and $\ell(s_kg)=\ell(g)-1$. This is the
exchange condition in geometric form. $\square$

So the fold applies exactly $\ell(g)$ reflections, and the word it records is
a **reduced word** for $g$. Its length mod 2 is $\varepsilon(g)$, the parity
bit. Which reduced word it produces depends on the order in which mirrors are
tested; the shader and CPU use the same order, so they agree.

**How many sweeps?** A sweep tests the three mirrors in order, so each sweep
does between 1 and 3 reflections while $x\notin D$. Two facts govern the
count:

* By Švarc–Milnor, $\ell(g)$ is comparable to the hyperbolic distance from
  $D$ to $x$, roughly a constant times distance.
* Near a vertex $V$, $2q$ chambers meet, and the longest element of
  $\langle a,c\rangle\cong D_q$ has length $q$. Only $a$ and $c$ fire there,
  alternately, at most two per sweep, so getting around a vertex costs about
  $q/2$ sweeps.

That is why the handoff's cap of 64 sweeps failed for $\{3,1000\}$ and why
the code caps $p,q\le32$: the measured worst case is about 35 sweeps at
$\rho^2=0.999$, with the view centred anywhere in $D$ (test
`fold_converges_within_cap_at_rim`).

### 6.3 Reading geometry off the folded point

The folded point $t\in D$ is the image of $x$ under a symmetry of the tiling,
so distances to tiling features are the same for $x$ and $t$:

* distance to the nearest polygon edge $=\operatorname{asinh}\langle t,n_c\rangle$,
  because within $D$ the nearest edge point lies on side $VE$;
* distance to the nearest vertex $=\operatorname{arccosh}(-\langle t,V\rangle)$.

**Exercises.**

## 7. The view, holonomy, and rebasing

### 7.1 The view matrix

The program state is one isometry $M$, mapping the **tiling frame**, where the
mirrors $n_a,n_b,n_c$ live, to the **screen frame**, whose origin is the
disk centre. A screen point $s$ is drawn according to the tiling-frame point
$M^{-1}s$, which the shader folds.

* Panning so that the screen point $p$ moves to $q$: $M\leftarrow T_{p\to q}M$.
  Left multiplication acts in the screen frame. The point grabbed at $p$ stays
  under the cursor, because each drag step carries the old cursor position to
  the new one.
* Rotating about the screen centre: $M\leftarrow R(\Delta\theta)M$.

### 7.2 Holonomy

Drag the cursor around a closed loop. The picture does **not** return to where
it was: it comes back rotated. For a geodesic triangle $ABC$ traversed
counterclockwise,
$$T_{C\to A}\,T_{B\to C}\,T_{A\to B}=R_A(-\operatorname{Area}(ABC)),$$
a *clockwise* rotation about $A$ by the triangle's area. I checked the sign
numerically. In general the rotation angle is the area enclosed, which is
Gauss–Bonnet once more. This is parallel transport around a loop on a
surface of curvature $-1$. It is not a bug, and it is a nice way to "feel"
negative curvature with the mouse.

### 7.3 Rebasing: choosing a coset representative

If $\gamma\in\Delta$ then $M$ and $M\gamma$ draw the same picture, because
$\gamma$ permutes the tiling. So the picture depends only on the coset
$M\Delta$. The program is free to replace $M$ by any representative.
`View::settle` chooses the one that puts the view centre in $D$:

1. Fold $c=M^{-1}O$, getting a word $k_1\cdots k_\ell$, so
   $F=s_{k_\ell}\cdots s_{k_1}$ satisfies $Fc\in D$.
2. If $\ell$ is odd, append $a$ (index 0). $F$ is replaced by $aF$, which moves
   $c$ into the neighbouring triangle $aD$. Now $\varepsilon(F)=+1$, so the
   two-tone parity coloring, which depends on the coset $M\Delta^+$, is also
   unchanged.
3. $M\leftarrow MF^{-1}=M\,s_{k_1}s_{k_2}\cdots s_{k_\ell}$.

Afterwards $M^{-1}O\in D\cup aD$, a compact set, so the entries of $M$ stay
bounded however far one pans. Then Lorentzian Gram–Schmidt (§2.5) removes the
remaining drift. Both are cheap enough to run after every input event.

**Exercises.**
1. Show that in step 3 the multiplication must be on the right. What would
   $M\leftarrow F^{-1}M$ do to the picture?
2. Bound the entries of $M$ after settling, in terms of the circumradius $R$.

## 8. Stable tile colors from a finite quotient

### 8.1 What is needed

By §5.3 we need a homomorphism $\varphi:\Delta\to H$ to a finite group, an
$H$-set $X$, and $x_0\in X$ fixed by $\varphi(a),\varphi(b)$. The shader
computes $\varphi(g)$ along the fold as a running product,
$\varphi(s_{k_1})\varphi(s_{k_2})\cdots$, and colors the tile by a hash of
$\varphi(g)x_0$.

For **stability under rebasing**, note that after `settle` the tiling frame
has been replaced by a new one, related by a symmetry $G\in\Delta$ that
accumulates over time. The CPU keeps $\Phi=\varphi(G)$ (`View::color_frame`),
updated by the same right multiplications as $M$, and the shader starts its
running product at $\Phi$. Colors then refer to a fixed frame and do not change
while panning.

### 8.2 Reducing the geometric representation mod $\ell$

The entries of $2B$ lie in the ring $\mathbb Z[\lambda_p,\lambda_q]$ of
algebraic integers, where $\lambda_m=2\cos\frac\pi m=\zeta_{2m}+\zeta_{2m}^{-1}$
and $\zeta_{2m}=e^{i\pi/m}$. Write the reflection as
$$\sigma_i=I+e_i\,n_i^{\mathsf T},\qquad n_i=-(\text{row }i\text{ of }2B).$$
Choose a prime $\ell\equiv1\pmod{2p}$ and $\pmod{2q}$, so that $\mathbb F_\ell^\times$,
which is cyclic of order $\ell-1$, contains elements of order $2p$ and $2q$.
Pick $\zeta\in\mathbb F_\ell$ of order $2m$, a suitable power of a primitive
root, and send $\lambda_m\mapsto\zeta+\zeta^{-1}$. The matrices $\sigma_i$ now
have entries in $\mathbb F_\ell$. **(code: `Quotient::new`)**

**Do the relations survive?** $\sigma_i^2=I$ always, since
$\sigma_ie_i=-e_i$. For a pair $i,j$ with label $m$, $\sigma_i\sigma_j$ acts on
$\operatorname{span}(e_i,e_j)$ with determinant 1 and trace $\lambda^2-2$,
where $\lambda=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}$. Its eigenvalues are therefore $\zeta^{\pm2}$,
of order exactly $m$. For $m\ge3$ they are distinct, because $\zeta^4\ne1$, so
$\sigma_i\sigma_j$ is diagonalizable with $(\sigma_i\sigma_j)^m=1$ on that
plane. For $m=2$, $\lambda=0$ and the block is $-I$. If $B$ restricted to the plane is
non-degenerate ($4-\lambda^2\ne0$, true when $\zeta^2\ne1$), then
$V=\operatorname{span}\oplus\operatorname{span}^\perp$, and $\sigma_i\sigma_j$
is the identity on the complement. Hence the relations hold and
$\varphi:\Delta\to GL_3(\mathbb F_\ell)$ is a homomorphism. The code does not
rely on this argument: it checks all six relations for each candidate $\ell$
and moves on if any fails.

**The code uses the transpose.** A point $x$ has *mirror coordinates*
$y_i=\langle x,n_i\rangle$. Reflecting $x$ in mirror $k$ changes them to
$y_i-2y_k\langle n_i,n_k\rangle=y_i+y_k(n_k)_i$, so they transform by
$$\sigma_k^{\mathsf T}=I+n_k\,e_k^{\mathsf T},$$
the contragredient of the geometric representation. Its relations are those
of the $\sigma_k$, transposed, so the same primes work. From here on $\varphi$
means this representation, $\varphi(s_k)=\sigma_k^{\mathsf T}$, and
$y(gx)=\varphi(g)\,y(x)$.

The tile centre has mirror coordinates $y(O)=(0,0,\langle O,n_c\rangle)$, a
multiple of $e_c$. Since $\sigma_k^{\mathsf T}w=w+w_k n_k$, the vector $e_c$ is
fixed by $\sigma_a^{\mathsf T}$ and $\sigma_b^{\mathsf T}$, but not by
$\sigma_c^{\mathsf T}$. **Tile $g\langle a,b\rangle$ gets color
$\varphi(g)e_c\in\mathbb F_\ell^3$**: up to scale, its centre $gO$ in mirror
coordinates, reduced mod $\ell$. For $\{7,3\}$ the smallest admissible prime is
$\ell=43$. **(code: `quotient::E_C`; the shader hashes column $c$ of its running
product)**

An earlier version used $\sigma_k$ itself, coloring by $\varphi(g)v_0$ with
$v_0=n_a\times n_b$. When $2B$ is invertible mod $\ell$ the two are isomorphic:
$2B\,\sigma_k=\sigma_k^{-\mathsf T}\,2B$, and $2Bv_0$ is a multiple of $e_c$. So
the color *classes* are the same and only the hash values differ. The switch
was forced by the Euclidean tilings, where $B$ is singular (§10.6).

**This is the elegant version.** There is no canonical word, $p$ and $q$ are
arbitrary, and the color of a tile is literally *its centre, reduced
mod $\ell$*. The real computation with tile centres $gO$ is a float
computation that cannot be hashed stably. The mod-$\ell$ shadow of the same
linear algebra is exact.

### 8.3 What the colors are: a finite hyperbolic plane

The $\sigma_k$ preserve $2B$, so their inverse transposes
$\sigma_k^{\mathsf T}$ preserve the dual form
$Q(w)=w^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{adj}(2B)\,w$ over $\mathbb F_\ell$, where the
adjugate $\operatorname{adj}(2B)=\det(2B)\,(2B)^{-1}$. So all colors lie on the
"sphere" $S_c=\{w:Q(w)=c\}$, $c=Q(e_c)\ne0$: the finite analogue of the
hyperboloid.
Facts about non-degenerate ternary quadratic forms over $\mathbb F_\ell$,
$\ell$ odd:

* $|S_c|=\ell^2\pm\ell$, with the sign given by a Legendre symbol involving
  $-c\det Q$;
* $|O(3,\mathbb F_\ell)|=2\ell(\ell^2-1)$;
* **Witt's theorem**: $O(Q)$ acts transitively on each $S_c$, $c\neq0$; the
  stabilizer of $v\in S_c$ is $O(v^\perp)$, a 2-dimensional orthogonal group
  of order $2(\ell\mp1)$.

Computed facts, from unit tests and a throwaway computation:

| $\{p,q\}$ | $\ell$ | colors $=\lvert S_c\rvert$ | $\lvert\varphi(\Delta)\rvert$ | $\lvert O(3,\mathbb F_\ell)\rvert$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| {7,3} | 43 | 1892 = 43²+43 | 79464 | 158928 |
| {5,4} | 41 | 1722 = 41²+41 | 68880 | 137760 |
| {8,8} | 17 | 306 = 17²+17 | 4896 | 9792 |
| {7,4} | 113 | 12882 = 113²+113 | 1442784 | 2885568 |

In every case I computed, the image $\varphi(\Delta)$ is **exactly index 2** in
$O(3,\mathbb F_\ell)$, and still transitive on the sphere. The index 2 comes from the **spinor
norm**, the homomorphism $O(Q)\to\mathbb F_\ell^\times/(\mathbb F_\ell^\times)^2$
that sends a reflection in a vector $w$ to the square class of $Q(w)$. All
three generators are reflections in vectors of norm $Q(e_i)=2$, so the image
lies in a proper subgroup cut out by spinor norm and determinant.

### 8.4 The finite quotient surface

$K=\ker\varphi$ is a normal subgroup of finite index. Its torsion consists of
conjugates of elements of the cell stabilizers $D_p,D_q,(\mathbb Z/2)^2$.
Since $\zeta$ has exact order $2m$, $\varphi$ is injective on those, so $K$ is
torsion-free. It therefore acts freely on $\mathbb H^2$, and $K\backslash\mathbb H^2$
is a **closed hyperbolic surface** tiled by
$N=|\varphi(\Delta)|/2p$ tiles, with Euler characteristic
$$\chi=N\Bigl(1-\frac p2+\frac pq\Bigr)\qquad(\text{faces}-\text{edges}+\text{vertices}).$$
For $\{7,3\}$ mod 43: $N=5676$, $\chi=-946$, genus 474.

The colors are coarser than these tiles. A color class is the preimage of
$\operatorname{Stab}(e_c)$, of order 42 for $\{7,3\}$ mod 43, which contains
$\varphi(\langle a,b\rangle)\cong D_7$ (order 14) with index 3. So each color
covers 3 tiles of the surface, related by the order-3 "extra rotation" about
the finite point $e_c$. That rotation exists in $O(2,\mathbb F_{43})$ but not
in $\Delta$. For $\{8,8\}$ mod 17 the stabilizer has order 16 = $2p$, so
colors are exactly the 306 tiles of a genus-307 surface.

Same-colored tiles are related by elements of $\Delta$ mapping into
$\operatorname{Stab}(e_c)$. Apart from the tile's own stabilizer, these are
typically long words, so on screen same-colored tiles are usually far apart.
The coloring looks random but is reproducible. Smaller $\ell$ means fewer colors and visible repetition. The
L key steps through admissible primes.

### 8.5 Variations to explore

* **Ramified primes.** For $\{7,3\}$ and $\ell=7$, $\lambda_7$ reduces to a
  value where $\sigma_a\sigma_b$ becomes *unipotent* rather than
  diagonalizable. Unipotent elements in characteristic 7 have order 7, so the
  relation can still hold. The famous quotient here is $PSL(2,7)$ of order 168,
  giving the Klein quartic: genus 3, tiled by 24 heptagons. Working out
  what the reduction gives at this ramified prime is a good exercise; the
  code only uses split primes.
* **Primes $\ell\equiv-1\pmod{2m}$.** Then $\zeta\in\mathbb F_{\ell^2}$ but
  $\zeta+\zeta^{-1}\in\mathbb F_\ell$, so the representation is still defined
  over $\mathbb F_\ell$. Supporting this would roughly double the choice of
  primes.
* **Proper colorings with few colors.** Colorings where adjacent tiles always
  differ, with a small fixed palette, come from small-index subgroups
  containing $\langle a,b\rangle$ but not $c$. They are much more special. See
  exercise 5.1 for $\mathbb Z/2$.
* **Canonical words** (ShortLex normal forms via automatic structures) are the
  other road, mentioned in the handoff. The quotient approach avoids them
  entirely.

**Exercises.**
1. Prove $Q$ is $\sigma_i^{\mathsf T}$-invariant. (Use that $\sigma_i$
   preserves $2B$ and $\sigma_i^{-1}=\sigma_i$.)
2. Count $|S_c|$ for a diagonal ternary form $x^2+y^2-z^2$ over $\mathbb F_\ell$
   by hand. Use the parametrization of the conic through a known point.
3. Explain the integer $\chi=-946$ by computing vertices and edges of the
   quotient surface directly.
4. Show that the color of a tile does not depend on which of its $2p$
   triangles the fold landed in. This is `color_is_constant_on_tiles`.

## 10. The Euclidean and spherical tilings

The $\{p,q\}$ with $(p-2)(q-2)\le4$ tile the Euclidean plane or the sphere.
Almost everything above carries over once the curvature $\kappa\in\{-1,0,1\}$
becomes a parameter. The code does this with one set of formulas
(`geom::Geometry`) rather than three implementations.

### 10.1 One model for three curvatures

Points are $x\in\mathbb R^3$ with
$$x_0^2+\kappa\,(x_1^2+x_2^2)=1,$$
and $x_0>0$ unless $\kappa=1$. That is the hyperboloid for $\kappa=-1$, the
unit sphere for $\kappa=1$, and the plane $x_0=1$ for $\kappa=0$. In every case
the origin is $O=(1,0,0)$ and the rotations about it are the same $R(\alpha)$.

A line is a **covector** $c$, the line being $\{x:c\cdot x=0\}$, with the
ordinary dot product. Lines are normalized with the form
$K=\operatorname{diag}(\kappa,1,1)$ to $c^{\mathsf T}Kc=1$. For $\kappa=-1$ this
is the old normal $n$ in disguise: $c=Jn$, $K=J$, and $c\cdot x=\langle x,n\rangle$.
The facts of §1.4–1.5 and §2.2 then hold for all three geometries:

* $c\cdot x=\sin_\kappa d$, where $d$ is the signed distance from $x$ to the
  line, and $\sin_\kappa$ is $\sinh$, the identity, or $\sin$;
* $c_i^{\mathsf T}Kc_j=-\cos\theta$ for lines meeting at angle $\theta$, so the
  Gram matrix $B$ of §4.3 is unchanged;
* the reflection in $c$ is $x\mapsto x-2(c\cdot x)\,Kc$ **(code: `normal`,
  `reflect`, the fold)**;
* for two points, $(x-y)^{\mathsf T}K(x-y)=\bigl(2\sin_\kappa\tfrac d2\bigr)^2$,
  the squared chord **(code: `dist`; the shader's vertex distance)**.

The shader once computed vertex distances as $\operatorname{arcosh}(-\langle t,V\rangle)$,
which has no $\kappa=0$ analogue and is ill-conditioned near 0. The chord form
is accurate in f32 down to the smallest distances.

Isometries preserve the point form $P=\operatorname{diag}(1,\kappa,\kappa)$,
$M^{\mathsf T}PM=P$, and the line form, $MKM^{\mathsf T}=K$. For $\kappa=\pm1$
either condition implies the other. For $\kappa=0$ they are needed together:
the first fixes the top row of $M$ to $\pm(1,0,0)$, and the second makes the
lower right $2\times2$ block orthogonal. That is the Euclidean motion
$x\mapsto Rx+t$ in homogeneous coordinates.

### 10.2 The picture

Stereographic projection from $(-1,0,0)$ gives all three pictures. It is
the default; §10.7 moves the projection point.
$$x=\frac{(1-\kappa\rho^2,\ 2u,\ 2v)}{1+\kappa\rho^2},\qquad(u,v)=\frac{(x_1,x_2)}{1+x_0}.\qquad\textbf{(code: `disk\_to\_point`, shader)}$$
For $\kappa=-1$ this is §3.1. For $\kappa=1$ it is the usual stereographic
projection of the sphere: the unit circle is the equator around the view
centre, and its antipode lies at infinity. For $\kappa=0$ it is the plane
itself, scaled by 2. The conformal factor is $2/(1+\kappa\rho^2)$ in every case,
which gives the shader's pixel size.

Only the hyperbolic picture has a boundary, $\rho^2=-1/\kappa$. As
$\kappa\to0^-$ the disk of radius $1/\sqrt{-\kappa}$ grows to the whole plane,
so the Euclidean view is a plane to pan over, not a disk.

### 10.3 Motions without the Lorentz group

The two-reflection translation of §2.3 and the Gram–Schmidt of §2.5 use the
non-degenerate form, which $\kappa=0$ lacks. The code uses the
**transvection** carrying $O$ to $x=(x_0,\mathbf x)$ along their geodesic:
$$T(x)=\begin{pmatrix}x_0&-\kappa\,\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\\\mathbf x&I-\frac{\kappa}{1+x_0}\mathbf x\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}.\qquad\textbf{(code: `transvect`)}$$
For $\kappa=-1$ this is the boost of exercise 2.3; for $\kappa=1$ the rotation
of the sphere in the plane of $O$ and $x$; for $\kappa=0$ the translation by
$\mathbf x$. It needs $x_0>-1$, which excludes only the antipode of $O$.
Then:

* $T(x)^{-1}=T(\bar x)$ with $\bar x=(x_0,-\mathbf x)$.
* $T_{p\to q}=T(p)\,T\bigl(T(p)^{-1}q\bigr)\,T(p)^{-1}$: move $p$ to $O$,
  translate along the image geodesic, move back. This is the same pure
  translation as §2.3 (`translate`).
* Every orientation-preserving isometry factors as $M=T(MO)\,R(\alpha)$. To
  remove drift, normalize the column $MO$, form $T(MO)^{-1}M$, which is nearly
  a rotation $r$, and take $\alpha=\operatorname{atan2}(r_{21}-r_{12},\,r_{11}+r_{22})$,
  the nearest rotation angle (`orthonormalize`). `settle` keeps $M$ even, so
  $M$ is always orientation-preserving.

A spherical pan can grab any point of the screen. $T(p)$ stays bounded:
$\|\mathbf p\|^2/(1+p_0)=1-p_0\le2$. Only a picture with a rim, the
hyperbolic disk or the sphere's horizon (§10.7), needs the clamp of the
implementation guide §3.3.

### 10.4 The triangle

$n_a$ and $n_b$ are as in §4.3. For $VE$ the angle conditions give
$$c=\bigl(c_0,\ -\cos\tfrac\pi q\cot\tfrac\pi p,\ -\cos\tfrac\pi q\bigr),\qquad
\kappa\,c_0^2=1-\frac{\cos^2(\pi/q)}{\sin^2(\pi/p)},$$
and $c_0>0$ puts $O$ inside. For $\kappa=\pm1$ the normalization fixes $c_0$; in
the hyperbolic case $c=Jn_c$, and $c_0=\sinh r$. For
$\kappa=0$ the right-hand side vanishes, because $\cos\frac\pi q=\sin\frac\pi p$
exactly when $\frac1p+\frac1q=\frac12$, and $c_0$ is free. The Euclidean plane
has similarities but no unit of length, so any size of triangle tiles. The
code picks $|OV|=0.6$, close to the hyperbolic $\{7,3\}$ and the spherical
$\{3,5\}$, $\{5,3\}$: then $c_0=0.6\cos\frac\pi q\cot\frac\pi p$. Vertices are
intersections of lines, the normalized cross product of their covectors
(`meet`). **(code: `Tiling::new`)**

### 10.5 Folding

The algorithm of §6 is unchanged. On the sphere $\Delta$ is finite (of order
24, 48 or 120) and the fold takes at most 6 sweeps over the whole sphere. In the
plane the number of sweeps grows linearly with the distance from the view
centre, not logarithmically as in the disk. It measured 20 at 4 disk radii, so
the cap of 64 is reached only in very elongated windows
(`fold_converges_within_cap`). Settling keeps $M$ bounded in the plane too,
where unsettled entries grow linearly rather than exponentially. On the sphere
$M$ is bounded anyway, and settling just keeps the color frame logic the same.

### 10.6 Colors

**The geometric representation fails in the plane.** In the Euclidean case
$2B$ has rank 2, with a radical $\delta$. A vector fixed by $\sigma_a$ and
$\sigma_b$ is orthogonal to $n_a$ and $n_b$. These span the row space, so the
vector is orthogonal to $n_c$ too, and $\sigma_c$ fixes it as well: the old
$v_0=n_a\times n_b$ is $\delta$, and every tile gets the same color. Mirror
coordinates (§8.2) do not have this problem. The $\sigma_k^{\mathsf T}$ act on
the affine functions of the plane, and $e_c$ is not fixed by
$\sigma_c^{\mathsf T}$, because $(n_c)_c=-2$.

* **Spherical:** for the first admissible prime $\varphi$ is faithful on the
  finite group, so the colors are exactly the tiles: 4, 8, 6, 20 and 12 for
  $\{3,3\}$, $\{3,4\}$, $\{4,3\}$, $\{3,5\}$ and $\{5,3\}$, the faces of the
  Platonic solids (`spherical_colors_are_the_tiles`).
* **Euclidean:** $\Delta=\Lambda\rtimes W_0$, with $\Lambda$ the translation
  lattice and $W_0$ the finite group fixing $O$. Mirror coordinates are affine
  coordinates, so translations act unipotently and have order $\ell$ mod $\ell$.
  The coloring therefore factors through the torus $\mathbb R^2/\ell\Lambda$.
  The count is $\ell^2$ for all three tilings (`euclidean_colors_are_a_torus`).
  For $\{4,4\}$ and $\{6,3\}$, $\Lambda$ acts simply transitively on tiles, so
  the colors are exactly the $\ell^2$ tiles of the torus. For $\{3,6\}$ the
  torus has $2\ell^2$ triangles, and each color covers two of them. Same-colored
  tiles repeat every $\ell$ lattice steps, which in the plane you can actually
  pan to and see, unlike in the hyperbolic case.

### 10.7 Other projection points

Projecting from $(-\lambda,0,0)$ instead of $(-1,0,0)$ gives a family of
pictures. The code parametrizes it by $\mu=\lambda/(1+\lambda)\in[0,1]$, so
that $\lambda=\infty$ is reachable. Write $a=1-\mu$ and $b=\mu$, and scale the
picture to agree with Poincaré's at the centre, where a distance $d$ appears
as $|\mathbf u|\approx d/2$:
$$\mathbf u=\frac{\mathbf x}{2(a\,x_0+b)}.$$

| μ | λ | hyperbolic | spherical |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | Klein: geodesics are straight | gnomonic: great circles are straight |
| 1/2 | 1 | Poincaré: conformal | stereographic: conformal |
| 1 | ∞ | Gans: the whole plane | orthographic: a hemisphere |

For $\kappa=0$ every μ gives the same picture, $\mathbf u=\mathbf x/2$.

**Inverse (code: `disk\_to\_point`, shader).** With $s=4|\mathbf u|^2$, the
point on the projecting line is
$$x_0=\frac{\sqrt{1+\kappa s(a^2-b^2)}-\kappa s\,ab}{1+\kappa s\,a^2},\qquad \mathbf x=2\mathbf u\,(a\,x_0+b).$$
The line can meet the surface twice; the root with $+\sqrt{\ }$ is always the
one in the picture.

**Which values make sense.** Since $|\mathbf x|=\sin_\kappa d$ and $x_0=\cos_\kappa d$,
the radius is $|\mathbf u|=\sin_\kappa d\,/\,2(a\cos_\kappa d+b)$, and with
$\cos_\kappa^2+\kappa\sin_\kappa^2=1$,
$$\frac{d|\mathbf u|}{dd}=\frac{a+b\cos_\kappa d}{2(a\cos_\kappa d+b)^2}.$$
The picture is one-to-one as long as $a+b\,x_0>0$, i.e. on the side
$x_0>-1/\lambda$ of a **horizon**.

* Hyperbolic: no horizon for $\lambda\ge0$. The whole plane fills a disk of
  radius $1/2a=(1+\lambda)/2$: 1/2 for Klein, growing to the whole plane for
  Gans. For $\lambda<0$ a horizon at $\cosh d=1/|\lambda|$ cuts it to a ball.
* Spherical, $\lambda\le1$: the picture fills the plane with the cap
  $x_0>-\lambda$, a hemisphere for gnomonic up to all but the antipode for
  stereographic.
* Spherical, $\lambda>1$: perspective from outside the sphere, looking through
  it at the far side around $O$. The horizon $x_0=-1/\lambda$ is the circle
  $\rho^2=1/4(b^2-a^2)$ (code: `rim2`), and pans are clamped inside it like
  in the hyperbolic disk.
* Spherical, $\lambda<-1$, is perspective from outside on $O$'s side: the
  globe as seen from space, showing the cap $x_0>1/|\lambda|$. It joins the
  family at $\lambda=\pm\infty$ but is not implemented.

**Pixel size.** Only $\mu=1/2$ is conformal. One unit of $\mathbf u$ is
$2(a x_0+b)$ long tangentially (circumference $2\pi\sin_\kappa d$ against
$2\pi|\mathbf u|$), and $2(a x_0+b)^2/(a+b x_0)$ radially, by the derivative
above. The two agree when $a=b$, giving $2/(1+\kappa\rho^2)$ again. The shader
uses the larger, so lines are thinner than asked in the other direction, but
never aliased (unit test: `length_per_disk_unit`).

## 11. Infinite p and q

With $\pi/\infty=0$, the fundamental triangle $(\pi/2,\pi/p,\pi/q)$ still
exists for $p=\infty$ or $q=\infty$, and the tiling is hyperbolic: the
condition $\frac1p+\frac1q<\frac12$ always holds. An angle of 0 means the two
mirrors at that corner are **parallel**: they meet only at infinity, their
Gram entry is $-\cos0=-1$, and the corner is an **ideal point** on the circle
at infinity. The triangle still has finite area,
$\frac\pi2-\frac\pi p-\frac\pi q$.

* $q=\infty$: infinitely many tiles meet at each vertex, so the vertices are
  ideal and the tiles are ideal regular $p$-gons. $\{3,\infty\}$ is the Farey
  tessellation.
* $p=\infty$: the tiles are regular **apeirogons**, with infinitely many
  equal finite edges. Their vertices lie on a horocycle, and the tile's
  "centre" $O$ is that horocycle's ideal point.
* $\{\infty,\infty\}$: both. Only $E$ is a finite point.

### 11.1 The frame

For finite $p$ nothing changes: $O$ is the origin, and for $q=\infty$ the
vertex $V=\text{meet}(a,c)$ is a null vector, which `meet` reports as `None`.
For $p=\infty$ the origin cannot be the tile centre. The code then puts $E$,
which is always finite, at the origin, with $c=(0,0,1)$ (the edge $VE$ along
the $x_1$ axis) and $b=(0,1,0)$ ($OE$ along the $x_2$ axis, $O$ straight up). The
Gram conditions $c_a^{\mathsf T}Kc_b=-\cos\frac\pi p$ and
$c_a^{\mathsf T}Kc_c=-\cos\frac\pi q$ give
$$c_a=\bigl(a_0,\ -\cos\tfrac\pi p,\ -\cos\tfrac\pi q\bigr),\qquad
\kappa\,a_0^2=1-\cos^2\tfrac\pi p-\cos^2\tfrac\pi q,$$
so $a_0=\cos\frac\pi q$ for $p=\infty$. $E$ lies inside $a$ because $a_0>0$. This
frame would work for every $\{p,q\}$, but for finite $p$ the tile-centred view
is nicer. **(code: `Tiling::new`)**

### 11.2 The fold near a cusp

Near an ideal corner a plain fold bounces between the two parallel mirrors.
Their product is parabolic, a translation along horocycles, and each pair of
reflections advances the point by one triangle around the cusp. The tiles
around a cusp have constant width along each horocycle, while a point's
horocyclic coordinate grows without bound, so the sweep count has no bound
either. In the Poincaré disk it grows like $2/(1-\rho^2)$: about 100 sweeps
where the rim fade starts at $\rho^2=0.98$, and 2000 at 0.999. Worse, panning
toward the ideal point puts the whole screen in the cusp.

**The cusp step.** Let $c_0$ be $OV$ and $c_j$ the mirror parallel to it, so
$c_0^{\mathsf T}Kc_j=-1$ and $n=c_0+c_j$ is null. Write $u=c_0\cdot x$ and
$h=n\cdot x$. Then:

* $h$ is invariant under both reflections, since $n^{\mathsf T}Kc_0=n^{\mathsf T}Kc_j=0$.
  It is positive on the whole plane, and goes to 0 deep in the cusp; it is
  the local width of the cusp (`Tiling::cusp_width`).
* $\sigma_0$ sends $u\mapsto-u$ and $\sigma_j$ sends $u\mapsto2h-u$. So the two
  act on $u$ like the infinite dihedral group on a line, with fundamental
  interval $0\le u\le h$, and $\sigma_0\sigma_j$ translates $u$ by $-2h$.
* The $k$-th power of that translation is $\sigma_0\sigma_{\ell_k}$, the
  product of the reflections in $OV$ and in its $k$-th image
  $\ell_k=c_0-k\,n$. That image is again a unit covector:
  $\ell_k^{\mathsf T}K\ell_k=1-2k\,c_0^{\mathsf T}Kn+k^2n^{\mathsf T}Kn=1$.

So the fold takes $k=u/2h$ rounded toward 0, and reflects in $\ell_k$ and then
$c_0$. That leaves $|u|<2h$ for at most two ordinary reflections. Rounding
toward 0 matters: with `floor`, a point lying on $OV$ with $u=-10^{-16}$ would
jump to the far side, and the ordinary reflections would bring it back to
$-10^{-16}$, forever. **(code: `Tiling::fold`, shader)**

**Colors.** In mirror coordinates (§8.2), the reflection in a unit line
$\sum_m\alpha_mc_m$ is $y\mapsto y-2(\alpha\cdot y)B\alpha$. So
$\varphi=I+(N\alpha)\alpha^{\mathsf T}$ with $N=-2B$, which for a mirror
$\alpha=e_k$ is the old $\sigma_k^{\mathsf T}$. For $\ell_k$,
$\alpha=(1-k)e_0-k\,e_j$, whose entries reduce mod $\ell$. The tile's color is
then exact, however large $k$ is. **(code: `Quotient::reflect_right`, which
takes any line)**

With the cusp step, the worst case is back to about 34 sweeps, and
`MAX_SWEEPS` is 64 again.

**How deep one can pan.** A cusp of $D$ is unbounded, so settling (§7.3)
cannot keep $M$ bounded there: $M$ grows like $1/h$ at the view centre.
Coordinates there are of size $1/h$, while the cusp is $h$ wide, so f32 keeps
only about $\varepsilon/h^2$ of that width. Pans stop at $h=3\cdot10^{-3}$,
which keeps 0.7% (`View::too_deep`). By then the triangles at the centre are
narrower than a pixel, so going deeper would show only the mean tone.

Pixels toward the ideal point are deeper than the centre, and the jump
amplifies their error. With $u$ and $h$ off by
$\delta\approx\varepsilon\|M^{-1}\|\,|p|$, the place $u-2kh$ in the strip is
off by about $\delta(1+|u|/h)$. So each pixel estimates its own resolution,
and the shader fades out lines, dots and shades where $h$ is below 3 to 6
times that error, a margin calibrated against an f64 fold. Such pixels near
an ideal $O$ lie in the central apeirogon (they are inside its horoball
$h<h(E)$), so they still get its colour.

**Elsewhere.** Outside the cusps the fold maps a pixel back by an isometry
of norm about $|t|/|t_{\text{folded}}|$, which magnifies its rounding to about
that times $\varepsilon\|M^{-1}\||p|$. Compared with an f64 fold, no pixel was
off by a pixel while that estimate was under 4 px, so pixels fade to the
background from 1 to 4 px, as at the rim. A lost f32 fold can end far out or
on the lower sheet; then only $|t|$ is a safe bound. Inside a cusp's
horoball a lost pixel keeps the colour it is known to have.

**The fan at an ideal vertex.** The tiles around an ideal $V$ are
$(ac)^mP$, $m\in\mathbb Z$, so around the vertex $gV$ of a pixel's triangle
$gD$ their colours are $\varphi(g)\,U^me_c$ with
$U=\varphi(\sigma_a)\varphi(\sigma_c)$. $U$ is unipotent, so the colours repeat
with period $\ell$. Where the tiles are narrower than a pixel, the shader
averages the colours of the tiles the pixel spans, about $px/2s$ of them
for a triangle width $s=d(t,OV)+d(t,VE)$ across the cusp: a box filter along
the horocycle. Where f32 has lost the place along the horocycle but not the
cusp, it averages a full period, whose mean is the same from any tile. That
is how the picture of a cusp can stay right even where the fold is noise.

**Lines and shades.** Near an ideal $V$, edges converge from every
direction, and lines capped at a fixed fraction of the tile would cover the
cusp in white. So for an ideal $V$ the cap is also a tenth of the cusp's
local half-width, $d(t,a)+d(t,c)$ at the folded point $t$. The global cap
needs a finite length too: half the edge is infinite, so it uses the
distance from $E$ to $OV$ instead (`Tiling::size`). The two triangle shades
of a tile likewise blend to their mean where the triangle's width across
$OV$ and $OE$ drops below two pixels. That covers both an ideal $O$ and the
centre of an ordinary tile.

### 11.3 Colors: the modular group

$2\cos\frac\pi\infty=2$ is an ordinary integer, so an infinite label puts no
congruence condition on $\ell$, and there is no relation to check. The
parabolic element has order $\ell$ in the quotient: $\varphi(ab)$ is
unipotent, and $(I+N)^\ell=I$ in characteristic $\ell$. The tile stabilizer
$\langle a,b\rangle$ of an apeirogon is infinite dihedral, and its image
dihedral of order $2\ell$.

$\Delta(2,3,\infty)$ is the extended modular group $PGL(2,\mathbb Z)$, acting
on the Farey tessellation. Mod 7 its image has order $336=|PGL(2,\mathbb F_7)|$,
consistent with the kernel being the principal congruence subgroup
$\Gamma(7)$. The quotient surface is then the modular curve $X(7)$, which is
the Klein quartic of §8.5 again, now cut into 56 ideal triangles with 24
cusps. These are
exactly the colors of $\{3,\infty\}$ (56) and of $\{\infty,3\}$ (24)
(`colors_mod_7_are_the_klein_quartic`). For larger $\ell$ the counts follow
§8.3 instead. For the primes I tried, $\{3,\infty\}$ gets $\ell(\ell+1)$ colors, a sphere of the
invariant form, while the surface has $\ell(\ell^2-1)/6$ triangles; at
$\ell=13$ each color covers two of them.

**Exercises.**
1. Show that the product of reflections in two parallel lines is parabolic:
   it fixes their common ideal point and no other point of the closed disk.
2. Show that $\sigma_0\sigma_{\ell_k}=(\sigma_0\sigma_j)^k$ for
   $\ell_k=c_0-k(c_0+c_j)$, first on $u$ and $h$, then as matrices.
3. Show that around each ideal vertex of $\{3,\infty\}$ mod 7 the colors
   repeat with period 7, the width of the cusps of $X(7)$.

## 12. Hyperideal labels

### 12.1 Past infinity

The Gram entry of two mirrors is $-\cos(\pi/m)$ when they meet at angle
$\pi/m$, and $-1$ when they meet at infinity. Below $-1$ they are
**ultraparallel**: they never meet, and the entry is $-\cosh\delta$ for the
length $\delta$ of their common perpendicular. Coxeter writes the label as
$i\pi/\delta$, since $\cos(\pi/m)=\cosh\delta$ for $m=i\pi/\delta$. The corner
is then **hyperideal**: the lines' covectors have a spacelike cross
product, a point beyond the circle at infinity, and `meet` returns `None` as
it does for an ideal corner. The code writes every label as
$\lambda=-2B_{ij}$: $2\cos(\pi/m)$, then 2 for $\infty$, then $2\cosh\delta$
(`Count::lambda`). The triangle exists for every $\delta>0$ in either
position, and for both at once.

* **Hyperideal q.** A tile's sides are complete geodesics: images of VE that
  meet no other side. Adjacent sides are $2\delta$ apart, and between them the
  tile reaches the circle at infinity along an arc. Cutting each corner off
  along its polar, the common perpendicular, leaves a compact $2p$-gon with
  all right angles. The tiles at a "vertex" form an infinite chain along
  that polar, translated by $\sigma_a\sigma_c$, which moves $2\delta$ along it.
* **Hyperideal p.** The tile centre $O$ is beyond infinity. Each tile is a
  **pseudogon** with infinitely many equal edges, whose vertices lie on a
  hypercycle, the curve at constant distance from $O$'s polar. $q$ of them
  meet at each vertex.

For hyperideal $q$, $\Delta=\langle a,b\rangle*_{\langle b\rangle}\langle b,c\rangle$
is an amalgamated free product, because no relation ties $a$ to $c$. Its
Bass–Serre tree has a vertex per tile, of degree $p$, and one per side, of
degree 2, joining the two tiles on either side of it. So the dual graph of
the tiling is the infinite $p$-regular tree. The tiles cover the
whole plane. What remains of the circle at infinity, outside their arcs,
is the group's limit set, a Cantor set.

### 12.2 In the code

* **Frame.** For finite $p$ nothing changes: $c_0^2=\lambda_q^2/4\sin^2(\pi/p)-1$
  is just larger. For hyperideal $p$ the frame is that of $p=\infty$ (§11.1),
  with $a_0^2=\lambda_p^2/4+\lambda_q^2/4-1$.
* **Size.** With no vertex, lines and dots are capped by the distance from
  $E$ to $OV$, as for $q=\infty$.
* **Fold.** No cusps, so no jumps. Away from the limit set the fold is short:
  at most 7 sweeps over the sampled disks of `fold_converges_within_cap`.
* **Funnels.** $D$ is open to the circle at infinity between the two
  ultraparallel mirrors, beyond their common perpendicular. That end, a
  funnel, is unbounded like a cusp, so settling cannot bound $M$ there
  either, and unchecked $M$ grows until even f64 overflows. Pans stop where
  an entry of $M^{-1}$ would exceed $10^3$ (`View::too_deep`).
* **Colours.** $\lambda$ must reduce mod $\ell$, which an integer does with no
  condition on $\ell$. So the labels are the integers $2\cosh\delta=3,\dots,10$.
  There is no relation to check. For small $\ell$ the reduction can be
  degenerate: for $\{3,i\pi/\delta\}$ with $2\cosh\delta=4$,
  $\det 2B=8(1-\tfrac14-4)=-26$ vanishes mod 13, and the colours are then
  $13^2$, as in the Euclidean case (§10.6).

A continuous $\delta$ would lose the colours between integers, and needs a
second control; see the implementation guide, exercise 11.

## 13. The half-plane and the band

The disk is not the only conformal picture. This section adds two more, each
tied to the view the way the disk is: the screen frame and $M$ stay the same,
and only the map from the screen to the surface changes. Each one turns a
one-parameter group of isometries into horizontal translations of the
screen: the parabolic motions about an ideal point for the half-plane, and
the translations along a line for the band.

### 13.1 The half-plane

Pick the null vector $\ell=(1,0,1)$. It is the top of the disk's rim. Then
$h(x)=-\langle x,\ell\rangle=x_0-x_2$ is positive on $\mathbb H^2$, and
$$w=u+iv=\frac{(x_1,\ 1)}{x_0-x_2},\qquad
x=\frac{(1+|w|^2,\ 2u,\ |w|^2-1)}{2v}\qquad\textbf{(code: `half\_plane\_to\_point`, shader)}$$
maps $\mathbb H^2$ onto the upper half-plane $v>0$, with
$$ds=\frac{|dw|}{v}.$$

* **Horocycles.** A plane $h=\text{const}$ contains the direction of $\ell$.
  It meets the hyperboloid in a horocycle centred at $\ell$. So the lines
  $v=\text{const}$ are the horocycles about $\infty$, and $\log v$ is the
  signed distance from the one through $O$.
* **The disk.** In terms of the Poincaré coordinate $z$ of §3.1,
  $w=(z+i)/(1+iz)$. This Möbius map sends $z=i$ to $\infty$ and $0$ to $i$,
  with derivative 2 at 0. So near the centre the half-plane is the disk at
  half the scale, the same way up and not mirrored. The null vector
  $(1,0,-1)$ would give the circle inversion about $z=-i$ instead, which
  reverses orientation.
* **Motions fixing $\infty$** are $w\mapsto aw+b$, $a>0$. In the coordinates
  $(h,k,c)=(x_0-x_2,\ x_0+x_2,\ x_1)=(1,\ |w|^2,\ u)/v$ they are linear:
  $$h\mapsto h/a,\qquad c\mapsto c+\tfrac ba h,\qquad
  k\mapsto ak+2bc+\tfrac{b^2}a h.\qquad\textbf{(code: `half\_plane\_similarity`)}$$
  The isometry group becomes $PSL(2,\mathbb R)$, acting by real Möbius maps.

### 13.2 The band, and Mercator

Locate a point by $\sigma$, the distance along the $x_1$ axis to the foot of
its perpendicular, and $\eta$, its signed distance from the axis:
$$x=(\cos_\kappa\sigma\cos_\kappa\eta,\ \sin_\kappa\sigma\cos_\kappa\eta,\ \sin_\kappa\eta),\qquad
ds^2=\cos_\kappa^2\eta\,d\sigma^2+d\eta^2.$$
On the sphere these are longitude and latitude. Stretching $\eta$ to $t$
with $dt=d\eta/\cos_\kappa\eta$, and keeping $s=\sigma$, makes the picture
conformal:
$$s=\tan_\kappa^{-1}\frac{x_1}{x_0},\quad t=\tan_{-\kappa}^{-1}x_2,\qquad
x=\frac{(\cos_\kappa s,\ \sin_\kappa s,\ \sin_{-\kappa}t)}{\cos_{-\kappa}t},\qquad
ds=\frac{|dw|}{\cos_{-\kappa}t}.\qquad\textbf{(code: `band\_to\_point`, shader)}$$

| κ | picture | $s$ | $t$ | conformal factor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| −1 | band, $\lvert t\rvert<\pi/2$ | $\operatorname{artanh}(x_1/x_0)$ | $\arctan x_2$ | $\sec t$ |
| 0 | plane | $x_1$ | $x_2$ | 1 |
| 1 | Mercator | $\operatorname{atan2}(x_1,x_0)$ | $\operatorname{artanh}x_2$ | $\operatorname{sech}t$ |

In terms of the disk, $s+it=2\tan_\kappa^{-1}z$ as a complex function: the
Möbius map that sends the axis's ends to 0 and $\infty$, then $\log$. So the
band is the logarithm of the half-plane, as Mercator's projection is the
logarithm of the stereographic one. Translations along the axis are
$s\mapsto s+c$, and the reflection in the axis is $t\mapsto-t$.

### 13.3 What does not carry over

* **No half-plane on the sphere.** It needs a real null vector, an ideal
  point, and the sphere has none. In the plane it is the plane itself:
  scaled to curvature $-k^2$ around $O$ at height $1/k$, its metric is
  $|dw|/(1+ky)$, whose boundary recedes as $k\to0$. The band does carry over,
  as the table shows.
* **No projection family.** The disk's λ does not extend. Projecting from
  $-\tau\ell$ onto a plane parallel to $\ell$ gives $(u,\ v+|w|^2/2\tau)$,
  from a Klein-like picture at $\tau=0$ to the half-plane at $\tau=\infty$.
  But boosts toward $\ell$ only rescale it, so every $\tau$ is the same
  picture zoomed. The disk's radial formula applied to $\eta$ gives
  cylindrical projections, but not Mercator's. So both charts are shown only
  for the conformal $\mu=1/2$.

### 13.4 In the code

* **Screen units.** The shader and `Geometry::screen_to_point` take the
  pixel in units of the chart's scale: the disk's radius, or the distance
  from the centre to the half-plane's boundary, $w=d+i$, and to the band's
  edges, $(s,t)=\tfrac\pi2 d$. Mercator uses the band's scale, which shows
  latitudes up to $\operatorname{gd}(\pi/2)\approx66°$.
* **Pixel size** per screen unit is $1/v$ and $\tfrac\pi2/\cos_{-\kappa}t$
  (unit test: `length_per_screen_unit_in_charts`). The rim fade uses
  $1-2/\text{len}$, which is $\rho^2$ in the Poincaré disk.
* **Drags.** A pan still carries the grabbed point along its geodesic. The
  secondary drag uses each chart's own motions: $w\mapsto aw+b$ through the
  grabbed point, with $a=v_2/v_1$, $b=u_2-au_1$, and the translation by the
  drag's horizontal part along the band's axis. Both charts limit drags to
  $x_0\le99$, the distance from $O$ of the Poincaré disk's limit
  $\rho^2\le0.98$.

**Exercises.**
1. Check $w=(z+i)/(1+iz)$ by substituting §3.1's $z=(x_1+ix_2)/(1+x_0)$.
2. Show that the curves $t=\text{const}$ of the band are hypercycles, at
   constant distance from the axis, and that in the half-plane they are the
   rays from $w=0$.
3. In $\{\infty,3\}$, the tile centre $O$ is the ideal point straight up
   (§11.1), so in the half-plane the tiling is periodic in $u$. Find the
   period in terms of the edge length.
4. Show that $s+it=2\arctan z$ is Mercator's projection, with the poles at
   $z=\pm i$.

## 14. The surface in 3D

Every picture so far is a chart: a map from the screen onto the surface. The
3D view instead shows the surface itself, embedded in Euclidean $\mathbb R^3$
with $x_0$ up, as a camera would see it. It is tied to the view like the
charts are: $M$ moves the tiling over a surface that stays put.

### 14.1 What is drawn

The three surfaces are the level set $Q(x)=1$ of
$$Q(x)=x_0^2+\kappa\,(x_1^2+x_2^2)\qquad\textbf{(code: `qform` in the shader)},$$
restricted to $x_0>0$ unless $\kappa=1$: the upper sheet of the hyperboloid,
a bowl with the asymptotic cone $x_0=r$ (slope 45°); the plane $x_0=1$; and the
unit sphere. The bowl and the plane are infinite, so they are cut off at a
distance `extent` from $O$: at $x_0\le\cosh(\text{extent})$ on the
hyperboloid and at $x_1^2+x_2^2\le\text{extent}^2$ on the plane
**(code: `camera::cap`)**. The sphere is shown whole.

The embedding is not an isometry for $\kappa=-1$. A tangent vector $v$ has
Euclidean length$^2$ $v_0^2+v_1^2+v_2^2$ but hyperbolic length$^2$
$v^{\mathsf T}Kv=-v_0^2+v_1^2+v_2^2$. Along a circle about the axis $v_0=0$,
so the two agree. Along a radius at distance $r$ from $O$ a unit of
hyperbolic length is $\sqrt{\cosh 2r}\approx e^r/\sqrt2$ Euclidean units
long. Tiles far out are therefore long thin spokes, about $e^r/\sqrt2$
times longer than wide. This is a property of the embedding, not an artifact.

### 14.2 Rays

The camera has a target $T$, a unit frame (right $R$, up $U$, back $B$) and a
half height $h$: the model length from the centre of the picture to the
nearer edge of the pane, measured at the target. Screen position $d$, in
units of half the shorter side, has the ray
$$x(t)=o+t\,r,\qquad o=T+h\,(d_1R+d_2U),$$
with $r=-B$ for an orthographic camera, and $r$ the unit vector from the eye
$T+hB/\tan(\phi/2)$ to $o$ for a perspective one with field of view
$\phi$. The origin $o$ lies in the plane through $T$ facing the camera, so
it stays near the surface whatever the field of view. That keeps f32 from
losing digits to a distant eye. In exchange, $t$ is allowed to be negative,
down to the eye ($-\infty$ when orthographic).

Substituting into $Q(x)=1$ gives $at^2+2bt+c=0$ with $a=Q(r)$,
$b=Q(o,r)$, $c=Q(o)-1$ for the bilinear $Q(\cdot,\cdot)$. With
$s=-(b+\operatorname{sign}(b)\sqrt{b^2-ac})$ the roots are $s/a$ and $c/s$,
without the cancellation of the textbook formula. For $\kappa=0$,
$a=r_0^2$ and the roots are where $x_0=\pm1$. The first root, in order of
$t$, that is past the eye, on the right sheet and within the cap is the
point seen **(code: `camera::hit`, shader `space`)**. Looking across the bowl,
the near wall may be cut away by the cap, and then the second root is the
far wall, seen through the opening.

Rounding moves the hit off the surface, so it is scaled back by
$1/\sqrt{Q(x)}$ before the fold.

### 14.3 Which side

$\nabla Q=2(x_0,\kappa x_1,\kappa x_2)$ points to where $Q>1$: into the
bowl, above the plane, out of the sphere. That side is the top. A ray sees
the top if $r\cdot\nabla Q<0$. From below 45° a camera outside the bowl sees
its underside, and its near wall hides the bottom. That is why the default
view looks from 50°. The underside is drawn dimmer and greyer, so that
the two sides cannot be confused.

The lighting is a Lambert term with the Euclidean normal $\nabla Q/|\nabla Q|$
turned toward the eye, and a light at the camera, a little to the upper left.

### 14.4 Pixel size

The charts know a pixel's model size analytically (§10.7, §13.4). Here the
GPU's screen derivatives give it: $p_x=\partial x/\partial\text{px}_x$
and $p_y$ along the pixel's two sides, of model lengths
$\sqrt{p^{\mathsf T}Kp}$. Their geometric mean serves where one size is
needed, as for the f32 checks of §9.

It does not serve for lines. At a grazing angle, or on a spoke, a pixel is
much longer one way than the other. A line drawn with one size per pixel
is then too thick across one direction and too thin across the other. So
the fold carries $p_x$ and $p_y$ along with the point: each reflection
$x\mapsto x-2(c\cdot x)Kc$ is linear, and is applied to them too. In the
fundamental triangle the distance to a line $c$ is
$\operatorname{asin}_\kappa(c\cdot t)$, whose change per pixel near the line
is $|(c\cdot p_x,\ c\cdot p_y)|$. That is the pixel size across that line, and
the edge VE and the vertex dot each use their own. Lines then keep their
width in pixels in every direction, as in the charts.

### 14.5 Moving the camera

The camera has five degrees of freedom, those of a camera that does not
roll: the target $T$, the azimuth and elevation, and the half height $h$.
The field of view $\phi$ is a sixth, a property of the lens. The mouse
reaches all of them.

* **Orbit** changes the azimuth and the elevation, with $T$ fixed. The
  elevation stops at $\pm89°$, where the frame would lose its azimuth.
* **Pan** moves $T$ by $-h\,(\Delta d_1R+\Delta d_2U)$ for a pointer that moves
  $\Delta d$ in screen units. What lies at the target's depth then follows
  the pointer.
* **Dolly** scales $h$. The eye is at distance $h/\tan(\phi/2)$ from $T$, so
  it moves along its line to the target, and nearer things grow faster than
  farther ones.
* **Zoom** scales $\tan(\phi/2)$ and $h$ by the same factor. The eye stays
  where it is, and the picture is magnified uniformly. Orthographic, it has
  no field of view to change, and scales $h$ like a dolly.
* **The field of view** in the panel changes $\phi$ with $h$ fixed. The
  picture at the target keeps its size while the eye moves: a dolly zoom.
  At $\phi=0$ the eye has gone to infinity, which is the orthographic
  camera. So that end of the slider is continuous with the rest.

Parametrizing by $h$ rather than by the eye's distance is what makes the
orthographic camera a limit rather than a special case.

### 14.6 In the code

* `camera::Camera` holds $T$, the azimuth and elevation of $B$, and $h$. The
  field of view and the extent are settings, like λ.
* The uniforms carry $T$, $hR$, $hU$, and either the eye's offset $hB/\tan(\phi/2)$
  or $B$. A flag in `cam_target.w` sends the fragment shader to `space`
  before anything else. The branch depends only on a uniform, so the
  derivatives in `space` are taken in uniform control flow, as WGSL
  requires.
* Tests: the camera frame is orthonormal and level, and from straight
  above shows the disk's way up; rays through the centre hit a target on the
  surface; hits are on the surface, shown, and on the sphere's near side;
  a ray over the cut rim finds the far wall; `project` inverts `ray`; a pan
  holds the target's plane; an orbit keeps the target and stops at the
  elevation's bounds; a dolly moves the eye along its line and a zoom keeps
  it.

**Exercises.**
1. Show that a perspective camera with its eye at $(-\lambda,0,0)$, looking

2. An orthographic camera looking straight down sees the Gans projection.
